# Client Communication and Marketing

This Firm Function covers everything the firm says to clients and prospects outside the advice itself: marketing copy, social posts, client newsletters, website text, drafted emails, a chat widget answering questions in the firm's voice, and any AI-generated summary of client testimonials or online reviews. AI reaches this work earlier and more completely than it reaches advice, because nearly all of it is writing, and writing is what these tools do most readily. The constraint is that the rules written about advertisements do not care which tool produced the words. Much of this page describes the SEC's Marketing Rule, which by its own terms applies to advisers registered with the Commission; a state-registered adviser is under the state's own advertising requirements instead — different rules, aimed at the same substance, which is claims that mislead and performance framed without the risks that came with it.

## Key ideas

### The rule attaches to what is said, not to what wrote it

The SEC's Marketing Rule governs advertisements by investment advisers, and nothing in it turns on how the content was produced. An AI-drafted social post making a performance claim is regulated exactly like one an adviser typed from scratch. There is no lighter standard for AI-drafted marketing and no argument that a tool's involvement changes what the firm is responsible for having said. That holds whichever rule governs your firm: the tool's involvement is not a factor in the federal rule, and it is not a factor in a state's either.

### Testimonials and endorsements keep their conditions

The Marketing Rule permits testimonials and endorsements, but only under specific disclosure and disqualification conditions covering things like compensation, material conflicts, and a written agreement with the person giving it. An AI tool that drafts a testimonial, synthesizes several client comments into one quote, or scrapes and rephrases online reviews into marketing copy is producing exactly the kind of content those conditions were written for. The conditions do not relax because a model assembled the words. If anything, this is the use most likely to produce a problem quietly: a tool asked to "write some social proof from our reviews" will produce something that reads like a testimonial without anyone deciding to publish one.

### Fair and balanced is where AI-drafted copy fails

The Marketing Rule requires that an advertisement not be misleading, including through omission — that material risks and limitations get fair treatment alongside results. AI-drafted marketing copy has a specific failure mode here. A model asked to write persuasive copy about a strategy's performance will foreground the favorable framing unless it is explicitly told not to, because that is what persuasive writing means. That instinct runs directly against what the rule requires. Copy produced this way needs the same fact-and-framing review that advice work gets, not a proofread for typos and tone.

### A wrong claim in marketing gets repeated at scale

A [hallucination](/glossary#hallucination) in marketing copy looks like a fabricated average return, an invented client outcome, or an overstated years-in-business claim — confident, specific, and wrong. What makes this different from the same error in advice work is reuse. A wrong number in a plan goes to one client, who may well catch it. A wrong number in a one-pager goes to every prospect who receives it, sits on the website for a year, and gets pasted into three other documents before anyone notices. The error is the same; the blast radius is not.

### The chat widget is communication with the public too

A firm-voice chatbot on the website, or a scripted AI reply tool answering inbound prospect messages, is still communication with the public for Marketing Rule purposes even though no person drafts each individual reply. This is the case advisers are most likely to overlook, because deploying a widget does not feel like writing an ad — it feels like installing software. The relevant question is not how the words reached the prospect but whether the firm is responsible for them, and it is. A chatbot that answers "what returns do your clients get?" has just made a performance claim on the firm's behalf.

### Marketing materials are records

Marketing materials fall under the same retention obligation as any other advertisement, and an AI-drafted one is still an advertisement. Whether the prompts and intermediate drafts behind it are records too is less settled, and turns on what they contain. See [Records and Documentation](/domains/records-and-documentation) for what the obligation covers, and [this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#records-and-documentation) for how long things have to be kept. The practical consequence for this area is that the working material matters, not just the published post — if a prompt produced a claim the firm later has to explain, the prompt is part of the explanation.

## Common misconceptions

- "The Marketing Rule only applies to formal advertisements, not casual social posts or a chatbot reply." What counts as an advertisement is broader than a designed campaign, and a performance claim made casually is still a performance claim.
- "If a person reviews the AI draft before it goes out, the testimonial and endorsement conditions don't apply." Human review changes who is accountable for catching a problem; it does not change whether the content is a testimonial. The conditions attach to what the content is.
- "Nobody would take a chatbot's answer as the firm's position." A prospect reading the firm's website has no way to distinguish the firm's voice from the widget's, and no reason to try.
- "Marketing copy is lower stakes than advice, so it needs less review." The stakes are different, not lower — marketing reaches more people, persists longer, and is exactly what an examiner can pull up without asking the firm for anything.

<Callout type="info">
At a larger firm, AI-drafted public content almost always runs through a marketing or compliance pre-approval workflow rather than an individual adviser's judgment about their own posts. That changes the job from "review my own draft carefully" to "route it through the existing approval queue and wait," including for content that feels too small to bother with — a LinkedIn post, a reply to a review. The queue exists precisely because scattered individual judgment is what produces inconsistent public claims across a firm. An adviser who finds the approval step slow for AI-drafted content is usually discovering that the firm's volume expectations and its approval capacity were set before drafting got this fast.
</Callout>
