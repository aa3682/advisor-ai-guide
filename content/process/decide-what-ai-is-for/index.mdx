# Decide What AI Is For

This is the first step in The Adoption Path, and the one every later step depends on. Before you look at a single AI tool, you name the actual work in your practice that AI might help with — not AI in general, but specific, recurring tasks with a clear before and after. That list is what step two checks against the rules that apply, what step four uses to judge whether a tool fits, and what step five's policy is written around.

## Why it matters

A firm that skips this step tends to work backward: someone tries a tool because a peer mentioned it, likes what it does, and only then asks what it is for. That produces two kinds of trouble. Either the use stays informal and undocumented — nobody wrote down that a chat assistant is drafting meeting notes, so nobody thought to check the data-handling questions in step three — or the firm writes a policy so broad it tries to cover every hypothetical use, which makes it hard to follow and harder to supervise.

Naming the use case first also protects you from the reverse mistake: turning down AI everywhere because one bad headline or one confusing vendor pitch made the whole subject feel risky. A specific task, held up against what it would actually cost to get wrong, is a much easier thing to reason about than "AI" as a category.

## What actually happens

### Inventory the recurring work

Walk through a normal week and list the tasks that come up often enough to be worth automating or accelerating: drafting the first pass of a meeting summary, searching past client notes for a fact, turning a data sheet into a slide, drafting a routine email, summarizing a long document before a meeting. You are not evaluating tools yet — you are just listing what the practice actually spends time on.

### Sort by who sees the output

For each item on the list, ask whether the raw output could reach a client or prospect without a person rewriting it first. An internal draft that a person edits before anyone else sees it sits in one category. Anything that could plausibly go out with light editing — a client email, a piece of marketing copy, a answer to a client question — sits in a different one. This split matters because [Handle Client-Facing Use](/process/handle-client-facing-use), later in the Path, applies extra scrutiny to the second category, and it is easier to sort tasks into it now than to notice later that a workflow quietly became client-facing.

### Weigh time saved against what could go wrong

For each candidate, make a rough estimate of two things: how much time or effort it would realistically save, and what happens if the output is wrong, incomplete, or based on the wrong facts. A task that saves a few minutes but could put an inaccurate number in front of a client deserves more caution than one that saves an hour and stays entirely internal. You do not need precise numbers here — the point is to stop treating every use case as equally low-risk just because it involves the same underlying kind of tool.

### Write down a short list

End with a short, ranked list: the two or three uses worth pursuing first, in plain language, with a one-line note on why each one earns its place. This list is what the rest of the Path works from. It should be short enough that step two can map the rules against every item on it, not so long that mapping becomes its own project.

## Questions to ask yourself

- What does the practice actually spend repeated time on, apart from the advice itself?
- Which of these tasks would embarrass the firm if the output were wrong and nobody caught it?
- Does any candidate task touch a client's account data, or does it stay in general or public information?
- Would a person still review the output before it went anywhere, or could it bypass review?
- If this use case turned out to be a bad idea in six months, how would the firm find out?

## Common mistakes

- Starting from a tool instead of a task, so the "use case" is really just "try the thing everyone is talking about."
- Treating every use case as equally safe because they all involve the same kind of tool, rather than judging each one on what it actually touches and who sees the result.
- Writing a use-case list so long or so vague that step two, mapping the rules, has nothing concrete to check it against.
- Leaving informal, already-happening uses off the list because no one formally "decided" to start them.

## Which knowledge areas apply

- [Compliance and Supervision](/domains/compliance-and-supervision): Every use case on the list eventually needs someone accountable for reviewing it, so it helps to know what supervision requires before the list gets long.
- [Operations and Back Office](/domains/operations-and-back-office): Many of the lowest-risk, highest-value candidates live in back-office work rather than advice itself.
- [Prompting and Review Skills](/domains/prompting-and-review-skills): Judging what a task would realistically save, and what could go wrong, depends on knowing what these tools are actually good and bad at.

<Callout type="info">
At a larger firm, this step usually cannot stay with one person. Pull in whoever already owns supervision or technology decisions before the list is final, since they will need to sign off on it later anyway. A larger firm is also more likely to already have pieces of an existing vendor stack — practice-management software, a CRM, a portfolio system — some of which may already include AI features nobody has evaluated as such. Include those in the inventory rather than treating "AI" as only the tools bought specifically for that purpose.
</Callout>
