# Vetting a Vendor

Use this checklist to check one AI tool, for one use, against the five things that decide what the firm may put into it, and to record your decision. It is the companion to [Vet and Choose Tools](/process/vet-and-choose-tools), which explains why the bar is set before you look at any tool; this page is where you write the check down.

## What this measures

This checklist looks at one tool, for one use. It does not compare vendors or produce a short list.

It produces no score. Each check has an answer and a place where that answer is written, and the result is a dated decision with a named approver.

## Before you start

Have three things in front of you: the [use-case list](/glossary#use-case-list) entry this tool is meant to serve, the [duty map](/glossary#duty-map) for that use, and the cell of the [data matrix](/glossary#data-matrix) it would fill — which tier of information goes in, and which kind of tool this is. The tier decides what counts as a passing answer.

Two ground rules before you fill anything in:

- **Read the tool's own pages.** Work from its terms-of-service, data-handling, and privacy pages, and any agreement the firm has been offered — not a summary, a review site, or a sales page.
- **Check it like any other tool.** An [advisor-specific platform](/glossary#advisor-specific-platform) gets the same five checks, in the same order, rather than an assumption that its product settles the questions.

## What to check

Work through the five checks in this order. Each asks what happens to what the firm puts into the tool.

| Check | Where to look | What a clear answer says |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Whether inputs are used to [train](/glossary#training) the [model](/glossary#model) | Data-handling page; plan terms; any agreement | Whether what you type, paste, or upload is used to improve the service, and whether that is a setting or a commitment |
| 2. The retention period | Data-handling page; any agreement | How long inputs and outputs are kept, and what ends that period |
| 3. Whether the vendor will sign an agreement acting as the firm's [service provider](/glossary#service-provider) | Account terms; the agreement itself | Yes or no, and what the agreement covers |
| 4. Whether [subprocessors](/glossary#subprocessor) are disclosed | A published list, or the agreement | The parties the vendor passes data to, by name or by role |
| 5. What happens to data on cancellation | Terms-of-service; any agreement | Whether you can take your data out, and whether the vendor deletes what it holds |

On the first check, a setting someone can switch back on is weaker than a commitment in the terms; write down which you found.

On the second, the period is the vendor's to set and change, so this guide prints none; read it from the vendor's current terms.

On the third, what matters is who can change the words: the vendor can revise a public terms page on its own, while a signed agreement binds both sides. For an [SEC-registered adviser](/glossary#sec-registered-adviser), [Regulation S-P](/glossary#regulation-s-p)'s [safeguards rule](/glossary#safeguards-rule) requires the firm's policies to cover oversight of a service provider, including how quickly it must report a breach to the firm — see [Vendor Oversight in this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#vendor-oversight). A [state-registered adviser](/glossary#state-registered-adviser) is under the FTC Safeguards Rule and its state's requirements instead.

On the fourth, a list you can read counts as disclosure. A reference to unnamed partners does not.

## What you found

For each check, write the answer as the tool's own pages state it, not your impression of them, and name the document it came from. Where a page is silent, write "not stated": that is an answer, not a blank.

| Check | What the terms say | Where it is written |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Training | Inputs are not used to train | Signed agreement |
| 2. Retention | Kept for a stated period, then deleted | Data-handling page |
| 3. Service-provider agreement | Will sign; covers this use | Signed agreement |
| 4. Subprocessors | Published list, by name | Public subprocessor page |
| 5. Cancellation | Not stated | — |

The example is a fictional note-summarizing tool, checked by an adviser named Pat Example for summarizing client meetings — client-identifying information. Watch the third column: an answer that sits only on a public page is one the vendor can change without asking you. Date the whole table on the day you read the pages.

## Your decision

Start from the tier in the data matrix cell. If the tool will handle [client-identifying](/glossary#client-identifying) or [restricted](/glossary#restricted) information, a signed agreement is required: a public terms page alone does not qualify. A "not stated" on any check is a question to put to the vendor before deciding, not a pass.

| Decision | When it fits | What you write down |
|---|---|---|
| Approve for this use | Every check has an answer the tier allows, written where the tier requires | The use, the tier, the approver, the date |
| Approve with a condition | The tool clears only once something changes, such as a setting switched off or an agreement signed | The condition, and who confirms it is met |
| Decline | A check fails for this tier, or the vendor will not put the answer in writing | The check that failed |

Then complete the decision with two lines:

- **Who approves.** Name the person in the firm who can approve a new tool. The approval refers to the use-case list, the duty map, and the data matrix by name, not "looks fine to me."
- **When it is checked again.** Set a re-check trigger: a material change to the tool's terms, a new use proposed for it, or a fixed review interval the firm chooses — whichever comes first.

In the example, cancellation reads "not stated," so the decision is approve with a condition: get the vendor's answer in writing before any client information goes in.

## What this checklist does and does not tell you

It tells you whether this tool, on the terms you read, clears the bar the firm has already set for this one use.

It does not tell you whether the tool does the task well, or how it ranks against another tool. It does not clear any use but the one you checked. It says nothing about what you owe the client — the advice is the firm's whatever drafted it, which is what [fiduciary duty](/glossary#fiduciary-duty) means here.

And it is true only on the date you read the pages. The vendor can change its terms, its subprocessors, or the model underneath without asking the firm; [Vendor Oversight](/domains/vendor-oversight) covers watching for that.

## What to do with it

Date the completed checklist, sign the decision, and keep it with the firm's other working files. It is the evidence the check happened, and the kind of thing an [examination](/glossary#examination) asks a firm to produce.

Keep each dated copy rather than overwriting the last. Where the checklist is a record the firm has to keep, how long it keeps it is set by rule rather than preference — see [Records and Documentation in this year's figures](/tools/this-years-figures#records-and-documentation) — and a state-registered adviser checks what its own state requires.

If what you found puts the tool in a different category than the matrix assumed — a business plan with no agreement signed, for instance — correct the matrix before anyone uses the tool. Then carry the approved tool, and any condition on it, into [Write the AI Policy](/process/write-the-ai-policy).

## At a larger firm

<Callout type="info">
At a larger firm, tool approval usually runs through a vendor management or IT security process the adviser does not own. Use this checklist to prepare what you bring to that process — the use, the tier of information, and the duties it touches — not as a substitute for it. The firm may already hold a negotiated agreement with the vendor, so ask for it before reading public pages. Approval of a tool for someone else's use is not approval for yours.
</Callout>
